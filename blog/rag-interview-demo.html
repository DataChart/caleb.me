RAG & Python

The LLM demo that paid dividends

Posted November 3, 2026

The job interview was going really well. I'd been prepared for each question they'd asked.

"What does RAG stand for and how is it used?" The interviewer asked.

I froze for too long, "I'm not sure. It sounds familiar, but it's not coming to me for some reason." I responded, my hands starting to sweat.

"That's okay, you've done really well so far, let's move on." The interviewer turned the page.

"Wait!" I interjected, "what does it stand for?" I asked.

"RAG stands for retrieval augmented generator."

"Oh, right yeah it's used to retrieve document information." I smiled, "are you hoping to develop your own RAG system?" I asked.

The interviewer looked to his right at the man in the suit, the one that didn't speak much. A flicker of a smile passed between them. "Something like that." The interviewer said.

"How familiar with GitHub are you?" The interviewer moved onto the next question and the moment passed.

One week later, I sat down at Rock Bottom Cafe. I gently put my laptop on the table.

"Thanks for agreeing to meet with us again, hopefully it's not too much of an intrusion." The man who had interviewed me said. I knew him by his first name now and, if I were hired, he would be my future boss.

"No worries at all, I'm super excited that you wanted to meet with me again." I smiled. "I noticed that the resume you had at the interview was a little out of date, so I brought an updated version with me today." I pressed the perfectly white paper with dense black text across the table at them.

Neither the interviewer nor the man in the suit looked at it, "I see you brought your laptop along, does that mean you have something to show us?" The interviewer asked, pointing at my densely-stickered laptop.

I looked at my laptop nervously, "yeah, you mentioned you might be developing some RAG systems during the interview, so I brought a demo with me to show you today. It'll just take me a few minutes to get set up." I opened the laptop lid and pulled out my phone and fiddled with it nervously trying to turn on my hotspot.

"So do you follow sports much?" The interviewer asked.

"Not much, I do go to the high school basketball games occasionally."

"Really! Which high school?" The man in the suit asked, suddenly alight with energy and almost startling me out of my chair. It was the only time he'd spoken since introducing himself a week earlier.

"The one right down the street, it's where I graduated from." I clicked run on the Python file already open on my laptop. I sent a silent prayer that there was a buddha out there that could prevent a hastily written Python file from crashing.

"That's where my son goes to school right now, he plays on the basketball team. Number 47?" He grinned at me, waiting for me to come back.

"Yeah I know him, he's on the varsity team isn't he?" I said, much more unsure than I hoped it seemed.

"Yeah!" He beamed at me and the interviewer, overshadowing us with his smile. I smiled back nervously before looking down at the bright green laptop screen. I turned my laptop around to face the two intimidating men.

"I webscraped all 247 public webpages of your website, extracted the text from 73 associated PDFs, and chunked the text into 150-word segments, I used a MiniLM sentence transformer, a ChromaDB embedding layer, KNN to score the chunks based on the supplied prompt, and LM Studio to host a Mistral model as the base." They looked at me like I'd spoken a foreign language. "Try it out." I pushed the laptop towards them.

## What was running on that laptop

Behind that demo were four short scripts that run one after the other, top to bottom in a single notebook:

  • scrape.py reads the site map, downloads every allowed page and every PDF those pages link to, and saves the text.
  • chunk.py splits all of that text into overlapping 150-word pieces.
  • embed.py turns each piece into a vector with a small sentence-transformer model and stores it in ChromaDB.
  • chatbot.py takes a question, finds the five closest pieces, and hands them to a local LLM in LM Studio as the only context it may answer from.

Nothing leaves the laptop except the scrape itself. The embedding model, the vector database and the language model all run locally. Here's every script, in order.

## 1. Scraping, politely

The first job is getting the website's text without being a nuisance. The settings at the top make the scraper slow and careful on purpose: a random 1 to 3 second pause between requests, a 10-second timeout, a 500 KB cap on pages and a 5 MB cap on PDFs, and an honest User-Agent that says it's a research bot.

Before fetching anything, it reads the site's robots.txt. If the site asks for a crawl delay, the script raises its own delays to match. is_allowed checks every URL against the rules for both this bot's name and the wildcard *, so a page is only fetched if both say yes.

Rather than crawling links and hoping to find everything, it reads the sitemap. WordPress sites publish a sitemap index that points to more sitemaps (one for posts, one for pages), so the function calls itself on each child sitemap and collects every <loc> URL.

scrape_site ties it together. It filters the URL list to the same domain, drops images and other binary files, and removes anything robots.txt blocks. Then it walks the list with a pause before every request, skipping anything that isn't HTML or is too large, and stopping completely if the server ever answers 429 (too many requests). For each page it strips the parts that repeat everywhere (scripts, styles, navigation, headers, footers, sidebars and forms) and keeps the remaining text if there's more than 200 characters of it.

A lot of what a utility actually knows lives in PDFs: forms, rate schedules, reports and handouts. So for every page, find_pdf_links collects the same-domain PDF links that robots.txt allows, and the scraper keeps a global pdf_seen set so a PDF linked from ten pages is only downloaded once.

extract_pdf_from_url streams each PDF in 8 KB pieces and gives up once it passes the 5 MB cap, checks that the file really starts with the %PDF magic bytes, and pulls the text out with PyMuPDF. If a PDF has fewer than 100 characters of text, it's almost certainly a scanned image, so it's skipped instead of filling the database with noise.

The last few lines run the whole thing and save the result to scraped.json. On the run in the notebook, the sitemaps listed 247 pages, and the scraper came back with all 247 plus 73 PDFs.

## 2. Chunking

A whole web page is too much to hand a language model as context, and too blurry to search well, so the text gets cut into pieces. chunk_text splits on whitespace and takes 150 words at a time, stepping forward 130 words each time so that neighboring chunks share 20 words. That overlap means a sentence that falls on a boundary still shows up whole in one of the two chunks.

Each chunk keeps the URL it came from. PDF chunks point at the PDF itself and also remember the page that linked to it, so an answer can cite the exact document.

That's 3,329 chunks in total: 1,412 from web pages and 1,917 from PDFs. The PDFs, which are only 73 files, produce more searchable text than the 247 pages.

## 3. Embedding

Next, every chunk becomes a vector. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 is a small sentence-transformer model (about 80 MB) that maps a piece of text to 384 numbers, so that chunks with similar meaning end up close together. It runs on the laptop's own hardware. The vectors, the original text and each chunk's URL all go into a persistent ChromaDB collection on disk, so this step only has to run once.

## 4. The chatbot

The chatbot is where retrieval meets generation. retrieve embeds the question with the same MiniLM model, then asks ChromaDB for the five nearest chunks. That's the nearest-neighbor search, and it's what decides which parts of the website the model gets to see.

ask joins those five chunks into one context block, caps it at 3,000 characters (about 750 tokens) so it fits comfortably in a small local model, and sends it to LM Studio's OpenAI-compatible endpoint on localhost:1234. The system prompt tells the model to answer only from that context and to say it doesn't know otherwise, and the temperature is set low (0.3) to keep it from getting creative. Every answer is printed with the source URLs it was built from, so you can check it.

Finally, a simple loop: type a question, get an answer with sources, and type quit to stop. This is the part I turned around and pushed across the table.
