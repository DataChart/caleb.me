Leadership & Books

My thoughts on Brené Brown's Dare to Lead

Overall, this is a great read on leadership skills and deconstructing false beliefs about vulnerability, shame, courage, and how your values make you a better leader. There were a few particular topics that really hit home for me.

Vulnerability

Brené spends a lot of the book discussing how most people have been raised to fear vulnerability. She encourages the audience to fill in the sentence for their household: "Vulnerability is ___." Phrases like "vulnerability is the first step to being betrayed" resonated with a lot of people. I answered with the phrase:

"Vulnerability is the last option once you've tried everything else."

I particularly appreciate her discussion about how engineers often try to engineer their way out of vulnerability with smart apps and message boards. She fights this idea passionately, and it's a sentiment I can agree with. As a Data Scientist, I am well accustomed to taking historical data and trying to draw conclusions from it, vulnerability included. It just doesn't work. There is no way to predict what your loved ones are going to do, and there's no way to escape the vulnerability when you truly love someone.

Trust is built one marble at a time

Many people only want to be vulnerable with people they trust, but building trust takes being vulnerable. Trust is also built in small doses, not large grand gestures: "one marble at a time," with small doses of vulnerability.

Brené also deconstructs the idea that being vulnerable means oversharing or being brutally honest. She says that leaders do need to open up to their teams, especially about their personal lives, but within reason. It doesn't work just for the sake of being honest or building trust, especially if you try it when things are already escalating. You have to build trust beforehand so that you can depend on it later, not in the heat of the moment when trust is already fractured.

Rumbling

One practice that really hit home for me is what Brené calls "rumbling." This is an informal meeting style that encourages questions and openness, and thrives on not being over-prepared.

Courage, armor, and a strong spine

The second section is about having the courage and bravery to lead. Many people hold back the truth because they think it is kind, but Brené argues that holding back the truth is not only unkind, it is bad leadership. Holding back can come in a few ways, including half-truths, talking behind someone's back, or promising to do something and then not doing it.

This is often just a personal defense mechanism to keep yourself from having to be vulnerable, or what Brené calls "armor." She describes it as heavy armor and a weak spine: we defend ourselves from the front with heavy layers of armor so that nothing can hurt us, but eventually something will surprise us and reveal our weak spine. Effective leaders have a strong spine and no armor. After all, leadership is about helping others, not protecting ourselves.

Living into our values

A big part of the book is living into our values and finding which values make us strong leaders. This heavily resonated with me. As a lifelong introvert, I was prone to thinking that introverts just weren't meant to be strong leaders. I realized just how wrong I was when Brené herself revealed that she is an extreme introvert, more introverted than I consider myself to be.

Brené really disregards the idea that introversion or extroversion has anything to do with being a strong leader at all. Instead, she grounds the discussion in which values you should be leaning into. She identifies faith and courage as her strongest values and leans into them as her foundation as a leader. She is able to use these values to assess where she is strong and where she is weak. Her courage empowers her to speak up when she feels that something is wrong, a powerful trait in a leader. But it is also a weakness, because she can often be distracted by unproductive discussions, since she can't ignore things that she believes are wrong.

Finding my root value

Brené encourages readers to narrow their values down to just two, which I found particularly challenging. I had to spend a lot of time thinking, mapping out, and discussing my values in order to decide which ones were most important to me.

I easily narrowed my core values down to love, honesty, curiosity, balance, growth, and freedom. However, after nearly a week of thought, writing out lists, posing impossibly difficult questions to myself, and asking friends, family, and colleagues about their values, I landed on the conclusion that I really have only one root value: love.

  • All of my desire for freedom, balance, and growth is rooted in self-love and self-compassion.
  • All of my desire for curiosity and honesty is rooted in my love for my family, my friends, and the world.

I really resonated with a line that I wrote while journaling about the topic:

"In order to do right by the unending love that others have shown me, I must love myself, and I must show the world unconditional compassion that will flow through me."

Accomplish, acquire, collapse, repeat

Brené ends the book with a very wholehearted plea for her readers to look at their lives and look for what truly makes them happy and joyful. Leaders often push themselves toward things that don't benefit their happiness: "Accomplish, acquire, collapse, repeat." A never-ending checklist of tasks that become meaningless once they're complete.
