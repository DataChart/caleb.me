Statistics

What 2 million RuneScape hiscores say about players

Source on GitHub

github.com/DataChart/Statistical-Analysis-of-OSRS-Hiscores

For Statistical Machine Learning I at Oregon Tech, I got to combine two things I love: statistics and video games. The dataset was the Old School RuneScape (OSRS) hiscores, the public leaderboards that track every player's levels, experience and achievements.

The data

I started from Luke Arend's open scrape of the top 2 million players on the OSRS hiscores. The raw file is about 1.5 GB: 2,007,339 rows by 198 columns, covering XP, rank and level in every skill, plus scores for clue scrolls, minigames and bosses. After deduplication, 1,999,625 players remained.

I dropped the rank columns, timestamps and usernames, then kept total XP plus every score column. The username was a tough one to drop, because it carries a hidden signal. OSRS has a limited pool of names, so very short ones (the minimum is three characters) tend to belong to old accounts.

The question

Can you predict a player's total XP from how many times they've killed each in-game boss, even though that data is extremely sparse? Most players have never touched most bosses, so the feature matrix is mostly zeros, and the target is heavily skewed by a few maxed-out accounts.

The model

I built a scikit-learn pipeline: fill missing kill counts with 0, standardize, keep the k best features (5, 10 or 15) by an F-test, then fit a random forest regressor, with linear regression as a baseline. A grid search tried 216 combinations of tree count, depth, split and leaf sizes, and bootstrapping, with 3-fold cross-validation (648 fits).

The best random forest used 200 trees with a maximum depth of 10 and the 15 best features, and it scored an R² of 0.76 on the held-out test set. In other words, boss kill counts alone explain about three quarters of the variation in total XP. An earlier run with a randomized search landed at 0.70.

Clustering for storytelling

I also ran an unsupervised clustering analysis with K-means. Clustering doesn't need labels: it groups players who look alike across all those variables. The result was great for visualization and storytelling, because each cluster becomes a kind of player you can describe.

What I would do next

  • Go back to a full grid search with wider hyperparameter ranges
  • Try gradient boosting, plus Lasso and Ridge regression
  • Use group-based imputation instead of filling with zeros
  • Log-transform the skewed target
  • Engineer features like total boss kills and low-level boss kills
